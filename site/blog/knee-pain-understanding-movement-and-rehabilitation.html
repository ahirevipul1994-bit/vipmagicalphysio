Knee Pain

Knee pain: understanding movement and rehabilitation

Knee pain is usually a strength problem wearing a joint-shaped disguise. Here is why the quadriceps matter more than most people expect.

By Dr. Vipul Ahire (Physio) · Published 2 October 2026 · Reviewed 28 October 2026 · 6 min read

When a knee X-ray shows 'wear and tear', it is natural to assume the joint is worn out and that pain is the price of using it. That assumption is understandable, and often wrong — or at least incomplete.

Studies comparing X-ray findings with symptoms repeatedly find a weak relationship between the two. Many people with visible joint changes have no pain at all. Many people with significant knee pain have modest findings. Something else is contributing, and that something is usually modifiable.

Why the quadriceps matter so much

The quadriceps muscle at the front of the thigh is the primary shock absorber for the knee. When you walk downstairs, it lengthens under load to control your descent. When you stand up from a chair, it drives the movement. When it is weak, that load is managed differently — and often less comfortably.

Strengthening the quadriceps and the hip muscles is the single most reliably effective non-surgical intervention for knee pain. It is also unglamorous, which is probably why people look for something more exciting first.

The activities that hurt first and last

Stairs, squatting, kneeling and rising from low seats are usually the earliest casualties. These all share a feature: they demand high force from the quadriceps at specific joint angles. They are also the tasks people most want back, which makes them good measures of progress.

A common mistake is to avoid them entirely. Avoidance reduces tolerance further, and the gap between what you can do and what you need to do widens. A better approach is to reduce the demand temporarily — a higher chair, one stair at a time, a rail — while building capacity back up.

What rehabilitation looks like

  1. Settle the irritable phase: reduce swelling, modify load, and keep movement gentle but present
  2. Build isometric strength: muscle contractions without joint movement, which are usually well tolerated
  3. Progress to dynamic strength: sit-to-stand, step-ups, controlled squats within comfort
  4. Add capacity: longer walks, more stairs, uneven ground
  5. Return to the real demand: the temple steps, the market, the morning walk

Each stage should feel achievable rather than heroic. If an exercise consistently leaves the knee more swollen or painful the next day, the dose is wrong — not the exercise itself.

When to seek assessment urgently

Seek prompt medical assessment if the knee cannot bear weight after an injury, locks or gives way repeatedly, swells dramatically within hours, is hot with fever, or if you develop calf pain and swelling.
