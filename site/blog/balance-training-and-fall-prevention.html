Older Adults

Balance training and fall prevention

Falls are not an inevitable part of ageing. They are usually predictable, and often preventable — if you train the right things.

By Dr. Vipul Ahire (Physio) · Published 8 September 2026 · Reviewed 8 November 2026 · 6 min read

A fall is often described as an accident. In reality, most falls in older adults follow a predictable pattern: a balance system operating close to its limit, a momentary challenge — a turn, a step in the dark, an uneven paving stone — and no reserve left to recover.

That framing matters, because a system close to its limit can be moved further from that limit. Balance is trainable.

What balance actually depends on

  • The inner ear, which senses head position and movement
  • Vision, which provides context about the environment
  • Sensation in the feet, which reports the ground beneath you
  • Leg strength, which provides the power to correct a wobble
  • Reaction time, which determines whether the correction happens soon enough

When one system declines, the others compensate — often effectively, until they are also stretched. Assessment identifies which contribution is weakest, so training targets the right one rather than applying generic advice.

Training that actually changes risk

Balance improves when it is challenged, not when it is protected. Supported single-leg standing, heel-to-toe walking along a wall, turning practice and stepping over small obstacles all create the controlled instability that drives adaptation.

The critical qualifier is controlled. Challenge without safety is simply risk. Start with a firm hand support, reduce it gradually, and progress only when you are steady and confident. Strength training should run alongside, because the ability to correct a wobble depends on having the strength to do so.

The home matters as much as the training

Loose rugs, poor lighting, slippery bathroom floors, low toilet seats, clutter on stairs and unsupportive footwear are all modifiable. So is the habit of climbing on furniture to reach high shelves. These changes are unglamorous and highly effective.

Fear is part of the problem

After a fall, it is natural to become cautious. But excessive caution reduces activity, which reduces strength and balance, which increases risk. This spiral is common and worth naming out loud. A graded return to activity — starting well supported and building up — tends to reduce both fear and falls together.

Seek assessment promptly if

  • You have fallen once, or had a near-fall
  • You feel unsteady turning, on stairs, or in the dark
  • You have vertigo with vomiting, hearing loss or double vision
  • You have sudden imbalance with weakness, slurred speech or facial droop — call emergency services
  • You have had a fall with a head injury, particularly if you take blood thinners
A note on this articleThis article is general education. Falls risk assessment should be individual and, where possible, conducted in the person's own home.
