Home exercise programmes: common mistakes

The exercises are rarely the problem. The dose, the timing and the honesty about what you actually did usually are.

By Dr. Vipul Ahire (Physio) · Published 6 August 2026 · Reviewed 9 October 2026 · 5 min read

A home exercise programme is the engine of physiotherapy. Sessions with a therapist are where the plan is set and refined; the days in between are where change actually happens. Which is why the most common reason rehabilitation stalls is not a wrong exercise — it is how the programme is carried out.

Mistake one: doing too much, too soon

Enthusiasm in week one is common, and it often produces a flare-up that destroys confidence in week two. More is not better; the right amount is the amount you can recover from before the next session. A programme you can sustain for eight weeks beats a heroic one you abandon after five days.

Mistake two: doing too little, for too long

The opposite failure is quieter. Exercises done gently, briefly and occasionally produce very little adaptation. Tissue needs a challenge to change. If nothing ever feels like effort, the programme is probably not doing much.

Mistake three: not mentioning the pain

People often under-report pain after exercise, either to be polite or because they assume it is expected. We need accurate information to adjust the dose. Mild soreness that settles within a day is usually acceptable; sharp pain, swelling or soreness lasting several days is information we should act on.

Mistake four: dropping the boring parts

The exercises people skip are usually the ones that matter most: the slow controlled ones, the strengthening sets, the balance work. Stretching feels productive and is easy to do; loading a weak muscle is dull and hard. Guess which one changes capacity.

Mistake five: practising in the wrong environment

If your goal is to climb your own stairs, practising on a flat floor will only take you so far. Practise in the place and the conditions where the task happens, with supervision until it is safe to do alone.

How to make a programme stick

  • Anchor it to an existing routine — after morning tea, before the evening news
  • Keep the set short enough that skipping feels unreasonable
  • Track it simply, on paper or a phone note
  • Review it every few weeks rather than running the same dose for months
  • Ask for a written summary if you cannot remember the details
A note on this articleThis article is general education. A home programme should be prescribed for your specific presentation, not copied from a general list.

Questions

It depends on the goal. Strengthening usually needs two to three sessions weekly with recovery days between. Mobility and balance work often suits short daily practice. Your plan should specify this rather than leaving you to guess.

Restart at a slightly lower dose and build back up rather than resuming exactly where you stopped. Consistency over months matters far more than a perfect week.
